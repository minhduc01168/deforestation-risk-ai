Abstract
Deforestation monitoring requires both reliable historical mapping and actionable risk signals that can be translated into operational priorities. This study presents a reproducible, grid-based pipeline to predict deforestation risk and generate warning zones for a pilot landscape in Gia Lai Province, Vietnam. We develop the workflow in K'Bang District and evaluate geographic transferability using Mang Yang District as an area-based generalization test. Public datasets are integrated at a 1 km × 1 km grid-cell resolution, including Hansen Global Forest Change (treecover2000 and lossyear-derived loss labels, 2001-2024), terrain predictors from SRTM (elevation and slope), rainfall features from CHIRPS (2000-2025), and Sentinel-2 optical bands and indices for the 2023 reporting year. We compare a baseline score with an interpretable logistic regression model and a random forest model. Performance is reported using AUC, average precision (AP), precision, recall, F1-score, and an operational capture-rate metric indicating the share of observed events contained within the top-ranked risk cells. On the provided train-test split, logistic regression and random forest achieve AUC values of 0.817 and 0.861, respectively, while cross-district transfer (K'Bang→Mang Yang) yields AUC values of 0.589 and 0.535. The resulting dataset, evaluation protocol, and explainability outputs provide a transparent foundation for district-scale monitoring and future extensions to broader provincial applications.

1. Introduction
Vietnam’s forest landscapes deliver critical ecosystem services, yet forest loss and degradation remain persistent in regions where land-use pressures, accessibility, and governance constraints interact. Operational forest protection benefits from two complementary capabilities: (i) robust characterization of historical forest loss and (ii) forward-looking risk signals that prioritize monitoring and enforcement under limited resources.
Risk mapping has grown alongside the availability of open remote sensing products and cloud geospatial platforms. However, applied studies often face three practical challenges: (1) limited end-to-end reproducibility (data sources, aggregation rules, and export logic are not fully specified); (2) validation designs that do not test geographic transferability; and (3) outputs that are difficult to interpret for non-technical decision makers.
This manuscript develops a reproducible district-scale framework for deforestation risk prediction and warning-zone generation at 1 km resolution in Gia Lai Province. K'Bang District is used for pipeline development, while Mang Yang District provides an independent geography for area-based generalization testing. All derived datasets and figures are produced by a rerunnable workflow (GEE for geospatial processing; Python for modeling, evaluation, and reporting).
Our contributions are: (1) a master grid-cell dataset that integrates historical loss labels and interpretable predictors; (2) baseline versus machine-learning comparisons under a provided split and a cross-district transfer test; (3) capture-rate metrics that translate model rankings into actionable priorities; and (4) explainability outputs (coefficients and feature importance) suitable for stakeholder communication and student-led research dissemination.
2. Study Area and Data
2.1. Study Area
The study area is located in Gia Lai Province in Vietnam’s Central Highlands. Two districts are analyzed: K'Bang District (pipeline development) and Mang Yang District (area-based generalization test). Using a 1 km grid provides a compromise between spatial detail and operational usability: grid cells are interpretable for patrol planning, reduce pixel-scale noise, and standardize predictors and outcomes across heterogeneous data sources.
The master table used in this pilot contains 6,000 sampled grid cells (balanced by design), with district counts: KBang: 4,000, MangYang: 2,000. Provided split counts: train: 4,788, test: 1,212. 
2.2. Datasets
All inputs are public datasets accessed and processed within Google Earth Engine (GEE), with derived tables and figures produced in Python for reproducibility and reporting. The pipeline integrates forest baseline and loss history (Hansen), terrain (SRTM), rainfall (CHIRPS), and optical predictors from Sentinel-2 for the 2023 reporting year. 2.3. Data Processing and Feature Engineering
Geospatial processing is performed in GEE in three steps: (i) define district boundaries and generate a 1 km analysis grid; (ii) compute outcome labels from the Hansen lossyear layer and apply baseline forest masking using treecover2000; and (iii) aggregate predictor layers to grid cells using summary statistics (mean for continuous rasters; band/index means for Sentinel-2 composites).
Optical predictors for 2023 are derived from Sentinel-2 surface reflectance composites. NDVI is computed as (B8 − B4)/(B8 + B4) and NBR as (B8 − B12)/(B8 + B12). These indices provide interpretable signals related to vegetation vigor and burn/disturbance signatures. Rainfall variables are constructed from CHIRPS time series as a 12-month total preceding the extraction period and a long-run mean annual rainfall climatology (2000-2024).
3. Materials and Methods
3.1. Grid Construction (1 km × 1 km)
A regular grid with 1 km spacing is generated for each district, and each cell is assigned a unique identifier. All predictors and outcomes are aggregated to the grid-cell scale to ensure consistent spatial support across datasets, enabling transparent comparisons and actionable risk mapping.
3.2. Outcome Definition (Forest Loss Labels)
Forest loss outcomes are derived from Hansen lossyear. We define a binary label indicating whether any forest loss is present within the 1 km cell between 2001 and 2024. To focus on forested landscapes and reduce label noise, cells are filtered using baseline canopy cover (treecover2000 ≥ 30%). An auxiliary field records the first loss year within each cell to support sensitivity checks and future time-based validation designs.
3.3. Sampling and Validation Design
The dataset includes a provided train-test split and a cross-district transfer evaluation. In the provided split evaluation, the pipeline uses the assigned split labels to compute discrimination and capture-rate metrics. In the transfer evaluation, models are trained on all K'Bang samples and evaluated on all Mang Yang samples to test geographic generalization under domain shift.
Sampling is balanced by design (approximately equal positive and negative cases) to stabilize model fitting and to make precision-recall comparisons interpretable. Balanced sampling improves method development but does not represent natural prevalence; therefore, probability calibration is recommended before operational deployment on complete district grids.
3.4. Baseline Risk Score
As a transparent benchmark, we compute a baseline risk score from standardized predictors (z-scores). The baseline is intended to represent a simple linear prioritization that can be communicated to stakeholders without model complexity, and provides a reference point for evaluating statistical learning models.
3.5. Logistic Regression (Interpretability Model)
Logistic regression is trained on standardized predictors to obtain stable coefficients and interpretable directions of association. Positive coefficients indicate increased risk with larger predictor values, while negative coefficients indicate decreased risk. Standardization enables direct comparison of coefficient magnitudes across predictors.
3.6. Random Forest (Nonlinear Model)
Random forest is used to capture nonlinear effects and interactions among predictors. The model outputs a probability-like score that is used for ranking grid cells by risk. Feature importance is computed to summarize which predictors contribute most strongly to the model’s split decisions, supporting explainability at a coarse level.
3.7. Evaluation Metrics and Operational Capture Rates
Performance is summarized using AUC and AP, as well as thresholded precision, recall, and F1 score. To reflect operational prioritization, capture-rate metrics are reported as the fraction of positive test cases contained within the top 5% and top 10% highest-risk grid cells. These capture rates provide an action-oriented translation from model rankings to monitoring focus areas.
3.8. Reproducibility and Implementation
The workflow is designed to be reproducible using public datasets and a consistent grid-cell schema. GEE scripts perform data access, masking, aggregation, and export. Python scripts load the exported tables, train models, compute metrics, and generate figures and maps. All intermediate datasets are saved in tabular form to enable independent verification of model results and to support future extensions to additional districts or years.

5. Discussion
This study demonstrates an end-to-end, reproducible framework for grid-based deforestation risk prediction using public datasets. On the provided split, both logistic regression and random forest outperform the baseline score, indicating that aggregated terrain, rainfall, and optical predictors contain meaningful signal for distinguishing historical loss patterns.
Transfer performance decreases when models trained on K'Bang are evaluated in Mang Yang, consistent with geographic domain shift. Differences in land-use dynamics, road access, local enforcement, and background forest condition can alter the mapping between predictors and loss outcomes. Even under this shift, ranking-based metrics (capture rates) remain useful because they evaluate whether the model can prioritize a subset of grid cells that contains a disproportionate share of positives.
Explainability is essential for stakeholder-facing risk products. Logistic regression coefficients provide directional interpretations under standardized features, supporting transparent narratives. Random forest feature importance complements this by capturing nonlinear signals, though it does not provide causal interpretation. For operational communication, coefficients and importance should be paired with maps of predicted risk and warning zones.
Balanced sampling improves stability but affects probability calibration. Before deployment, risk probabilities should be recalibrated using prevalence-consistent samples or complete-grid inference with post-hoc calibration. Future work should add time-based validation using loss_first_year to evaluate early-warning behavior and incorporate additional predictors (distance to roads/settlements, protected areas, land tenure, and proximity to agriculture).
6. Conclusions
We present a reproducible deforestation risk prediction pipeline for Gia Lai Province, Vietnam, implemented at 1 km resolution and evaluated across two districts. Using Hansen forest loss history, SRTM terrain, CHIRPS rainfall, and Sentinel-2 optical features, the pipeline produces a master grid-cell dataset, interpretable models, and operational ranking metrics suitable for district-scale monitoring. The methods and evaluation protocol are structured to support transparent replication and extension to broader provincial applications.

Appendix A. Feature List and Definitions
treecover2000: Baseline tree canopy cover percentage in year 2000 (Hansen).
loss_any_2001_2024: Binary indicator of any forest loss within the grid cell during 2001-2024 (Hansen lossyear aggregation).
mean_elevation_m: Mean elevation (meters) from SRTM aggregated to the 1 km grid cell.
mean_slope_deg: Mean slope (degrees) from SRTM aggregated to the 1 km grid cell.
rain_last12m_total_mm: Total rainfall (mm) over the 12 months preceding the extraction period from CHIRPS.
rain_mean_annual_2000_2024_mm: Long-run mean annual rainfall (mm) computed from CHIRPS 2000-2024 time series.
Sentinel-2 bands (2023 composite): B2 (blue), B3 (green), B4 (red), B8 (NIR), B11 and B12 (SWIR).
NDVI: Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (B8 − B4)/(B8 + B4).
NBR: Normalized Burn Ratio (B8 − B12)/(B8 + B12).
risk_baseline, risk_logreg, risk_rf: Model risk scores used for ranking grid cells.
warn_top5_rf, warn_top10_rf: Binary flags indicating whether a grid cell is within the top 5% or top 10% of RF risk scores, respectively.
